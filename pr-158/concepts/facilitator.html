The facilitator ​

x402 makes an ordinary HTTP request payable: a client that calls a paid endpoint gets back 402 Payment Required, signs a payment, and retries with it. The facilitator is the HTTP service that turns that signed payment into a real, settled payment on the Canton Network — so your server never has to speak to a ledger, hold a wallet, or run a node. Your server (the resource server) just hands each payment to the facilitator, which does exactly two things with it: POST /v2/verify checks a signed payment against your terms without submitting or reserving anything; POST /v2/settle then executes it on Canton and returns the update id.

Non-custodial by construction ​

Canton settles in three steps — prepare, sign, execute — and that split is what makes the facilitator non-custodial. The payer does prepare and sign with its own key, without going through the facilitator. The facilitator only ever performs the final execute of a transaction the payer already signed: it never sees a private key, and it cannot move funds without that signature.

The payment scheme ​

  • exact on a canton:* network — the payer transfers Canton Coin (Amulet) directly to your party; you receive CC.

GET /v2/supported is the authority on what a given facilitator accepts — the network id and the asset to pay in.

How you get paid ​

Settlements land directly at your Canton party — the payout party of the project your API key belongs to. The payer transfers Amulet (CC) straight to it; the facilitator holds no balance, so there is nothing to withdraw. That requires your payout party to hold a live TransferPreapproval. Without one, Amulet cannot pay it directly — the Token Standard turns the transfer into an offer the party must accept — so the facilitator refuses the payment rather than relaying one that lands somewhere the payer can still withdraw it (see What settlement does). Completed settlements show up on the Settlements page of the merchant dashboard.

Your API key is the facilitator credential ​

A key you create on the API keys page of the merchant dashboard is what your server sends as Authorization: Bearer to /v2/verify and /v2/settle. You can create and rotate keys in the dashboard yourself — no facilitator redeploy — and the key itself is never stored: what is kept is its hash, plus its sk_live_/sk_test_ prefix and last four characters so you can tell your keys apart. A revocation can take up to a couple of minutes to take effect.

What verify guarantees ​

Steps 5 and 6 of the payment flow are the facilitator's half. Before any money moves, verify enforces a handful of guarantees — your payment can't be replayed, tampered with, or under-paid — and settle re-runs all of them before executing on Canton. The facilitator never moves a payer's funds on its own authority: the only write that moves them is executeAndWaitForTransaction of a payer-signed transaction.

These run in order, with no chain write. The first seven are shared code — the same @chainsafe/x402-core verifier the SDK exposes — so a payer can pre-flight its payload against them, and they refuse with the Canton scheme spec's codes, saying in extensions.detail which of a code's causes it was. The last is the facilitator's alone, since only it holds the state involved:

  1. Right scheme and network — the payload, the requirements, and the network this facilitator is bound to all agree (unsupported_scheme or invalid_network otherwise).
  2. The transaction decodes — preparedTransaction is one gzip member within the size caps, its node graph is well-formed, and it holds the one transfer. Its sender is the payer (the payload names none), and checks 3 to 6 report it. A payload missing its proof is invalid_exact_canton_missing_proof, and one that does not decode is invalid_exact_canton_malformed_payload.
  3. Still executable — the signed transfer's own executeBefore is far enough in the future to settle (invalid_exact_canton_expired). The quote itself no longer expires; the deadline that counts is inside what the payer signed.
  4. The keys belong to the payer — the payload carries none. The facilitator reads the sender party's signing keys from the ledger, by that party id, and it is the lookup that binds them to the payer, so a caller can't sign as a party it doesn't control (invalid_exact_canton_signature_invalid when none can be resolved).
  5. The signature is valid — the hash recomputed from the transaction itself must equal preparedTxHash, and the payer's key must have signed it (invalid_exact_canton_signature_invalid otherwise).
  6. The signed transfer actually pays you. This is what binds the payment to your terms: the transfer's receiver, instrument and amount are checked against the requirements, so a valid signature over a divergent transfer is rejected: invalid_exact_canton_merchant_mismatch, invalid_exact_canton_instrument_id_mismatch, invalid_exact_canton_amount_mismatch or invalid_exact_canton_memo_mismatch. The amount must be EQUAL, not merely sufficient — exact is the scheme's distinguishing property, and a payer who overpays has no refund path. The transfer's x402.memo must equal your extra.memo exactly — in both directions, so a transfer carrying a memo you did not quote is refused as well.
  7. The transfer went through a real factory — the contract the transfer choice was exercised on must be one the transaction itself discloses, exercised through the Token Standard TransferFactory interface. For Canton Coin it must be the DSO's ExternalPartyAmuletRules, signed by the instrument's admin alone. For a registry token (USDCx, say), when the facilitator verifies it against the registrars it is configured for, it must also be your own registry TransferPreapproval: signed by payTo alone, naming payTo as receiver and the instrument's admin, for a configured registrar (invalid_exact_canton_malformed_payload, with a factory_ detail naming which part failed). Without it a payer could exercise a contract of their own that merely exposes a choice of the same name, and the payment would reduce to "something called transfer happened". The disclosure travels inside what the payer signed, so this check takes it on the payer's word; the synchronizer authenticates it against the contract id at submission, which is where a fabricated one fails.
  8. No replay, and you're an allowed destination — the payment has not settled before (the ledger is the real guard: a replayed transfer names input holdings that are already spent), and for an authenticated merchant payTo must be a party that key is allowed to receive at (your destination policy). A destination denial comes back as invalid_exact_canton_merchant_not_registered with a destination_not_allowed detail, on verify and on settle alike.

What settlement does ​

Funds are real Splice Amulet (Canton Coin), transferred through the Token Standard's TransferFactory in one atomic Canton transaction. Your party must hold a live TransferPreapproval: with one the transfer is direct, so nothing is escrowed or locked up. Without one the Token Standard would make it an offer that completes only once you accept it — which is not a payment the facilitator can stand behind, since the resource is already served while the funds sit where the payer can withdraw them. Such a payment is refused at verify, with invalid_exact_canton_preapproval_missing. A registry token (USDCx, say) needs your registry TransferPreapproval the same way. When the facilitator verifies it against the registrars it is configured for, a payment without one is refused earlier, at the factory check (7 above), as invalid_exact_canton_malformed_payload with the detail factory_not_preapproval. What is recorded is the signed transfer's amount, not the quoted floor, so an overpayment is not understated.
